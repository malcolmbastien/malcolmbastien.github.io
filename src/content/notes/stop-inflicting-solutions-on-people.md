> The single most fundamental error of the last three decades is to try and design an idealised future state rather than working the evolutionary potential of the here and now.
>
> — Dave Snowden

Someone sees long delivery times, poor quality, or disengaged teams. They already know the answer. Maybe it's a new framework. Maybe it's a reorganisation. Maybe it's a tool.

The answer gets rolled out before anyone has taken the time to understand the work. Teams learn a new language, fill in new fields, attend new ceremonies, and try to meet a new target. The actual constraints remain in place.

This is the solution trap. In a complex adaptive system, bringing in a method from outside does not guarantee improvement. The intervention changes the system. It can add work, shift incentives, and make the organization feel busy while the condition that matters stays untouched.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A solution becomes useful when it helps people see and change a problem they recognize. Without that connection, adoption is activity, not improvement.

## The Problem Starts With Someone Else's Solution

> I think like one of the key things to remember is you should never ask teams to solve problems they don't have, or they can't see. The only way to learn is to close a feedback loop by saying, 'I can see this problem. I'm going to apply this tool to this problem, and I'm going to see that the problem goes away.'
>
> That's learning. You can close that feedback loop. And in too many organisations, what I see is people bring in external tools, external methods, external processes, giant frameworks, and they impose them upon teams holistically. Like, "You have to do all of this stuff." And teams ends up being like, "I don't know what half of this stuff is, I don't know what the problem is, and I don't see anything changing. Because I didn't have any of the problems, or at least I could not see many of the problems that these tools are intended to solve."
>
> — Jabe Bloom

A tool is not the problem. The problem starts when someone says, "Use this" before asking, "What are you trying to change?"

The tool may be well designed. It may work for another team, in another context, with a different problem. That does not make it useful here. If the team cannot see the problem, adopting the tool gives them a practice to perform but no clear condition to improve.

It also gives them a second problem. They still have the original constraint, and now they have a new set of practices, expectations, and reporting obligations. The intervention consumes attention without necessarily improving the work.

## The Solution Becomes the Work

Once an organization has chosen its future state, people spend trust and political capital defending it. The more expensive the rollout, the harder it becomes to say that the answer may be wrong. The people closest to the work can see the mismatch, but the organization has made questioning the target feel like questioning the whole effort.

> In complex systems obvious solutions often fail to produce intended results — in fact, often they exacerbate the very problems they are intended to resolve.
>
> — Peter Senge

The result is often a system that measures activity more reliably than it measures improvement. Teams attend the meetings, complete the artifacts, update the fields, and report adoption. Those things are visible. The condition that prompted the change may remain hidden.

This can make things worse. New practices create coordination, new handoffs, and new queues. Teams spend less time talking to customers and more time proving that they are following the process. The official process looks cleaner while the real work moves around it.

When a solution becomes the work, the people doing the work stop asking whether it helps. They start asking how to comply.

## Start With the Work People Are Actually Doing

An answer imposed from a distance is a guess. The people doing the work know things the plan cannot show: where work waits, which dependency gets negotiated, which workaround keeps a release moving, and what customers complain about when the process looks fine on paper.

Spend time there. Watch how the work moves. Ask what people do when the official process fails. Map the current flow. This is work-as-done: the living system beneath the plan. It is not perfect, but it is the reality any intervention has to fit.

> Systems thinking focuses on closing the gap to a future state. Complexity thinking focuses on changing the present.
>
> — Dave Snowden

Present thinking starts with a description of what is happening and an honest account of what can be influenced from there. It keeps a direction without pretending the destination is known. Make a small change. Observe the result. Use what you learn to decide what to do next.

The useful questions change with it:

- Where are we right now?
- What are we paying attention to?
- What problem are we actually trying to solve?
- What can we influence in the present?
- How will we know whether the change helped?

The question "What do we want to happen?" should remain subordinate to the question "What is happening?"

## Kanban Makes the Present Visible

Kanban is useful here because it starts with observation rather than prescription. It does not require a particular team structure, meeting cadence, set of roles, or reorganisation. It gives people a way to see the work they are already doing.

> The essence of starting with Kanban is to change as little as possible.
>
> — David Anderson

The starting point is the current process as it is actually practiced. Visualize it. See where work goes. See where it gets stuck.

> If you don't know what else to do, make it visual. Find some way to make what's happening visual, so you can see the flow.
>
> — John Shook

From there, Kanban offers practices rather than a single prescribed process:

- **Visualize:** make the flow of work visible.
- **Limit Work-In-Progress:** reduce the amount of work in flight.
- **Manage Flow:** look at how work moves and where it waits.
- **Implement Feedback Loops:** learn from what happens after a change.
- **Make Policies Explicit:** record the decisions that shape the work.
- **Improve Collaboratively:** change the system with the people who use it.

These practices do not tell the team which problem matters most. They give the team a shared way to notice the problem and discuss what to try.

> Kanban was a gift that emerged from a focus on culture rather than a managed transition to a defined process definition.
>
> — Lessons in Agile Management

## From Seeing the Work to Evolving It

Once the current state is visible, improvement can be small and evidence-based.

If too much work is in progress, try a limit. If a handoff waits, talk to the people on both sides. If quality drops before release, shorten the batch and bring feedback forward. The point is not the tool. The point is the loop: observe a condition, choose a change, try it, see what happens, and adjust.

> When organizations see the interconnectedness of various departments and processes, they make better decisions, work together in more collaborative ways, and avoid the common and costly trap of suboptimization.
>
> — Value Stream Mapping

Leadership's role changes with the work. Spend time where the work happens. Help teams develop a shared view of the present. Give them the authority to choose an experiment. Protect the feedback that tells you whether it helped.

A transformation built this way does not produce one uniform process. It produces a system that can learn.

## The Test Before You Impose a Solution

Before introducing a tool, method, or framework, ask:

1. What problem can the people closest to the work see?
2. What evidence shows that this problem exists?
3. What small change could test whether the tool or practice helps?
4. How will we know whether the condition changed?
5. What will we do with the answer if the change does not help?

Without those answers, the solution is a bet. Sometimes the bet will be right. The cost is that an ungrounded answer still consumes the team's time, attention, and trust.

A better way of working emerges when people can see the work clearly enough to improve it. Start with what they do now. Work with the adaptations already there. Make one change. Close the loop. Let the next step emerge from what the team learned.
