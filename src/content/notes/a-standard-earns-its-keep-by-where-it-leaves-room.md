## Agile and Standards Are Not Opposites

Agile frameworks and standardization do not conflict.

The Agile Manifesto standardizes values. Scrum standardizes team operational schedules. Lean standardizes value streams.

The debate is not about whether to standardize. The real choice is which level of the system to fix:

- Principles
- Practices
- Outcomes
- Operating structure
- The process for making changes

Each framework standardizes a different level and leaves the remaining levels variable. Most organizational debates about agile stem from this difference.

## What Each Framework Standardizes

**Scrum standardizes the operational format.** It defines three roles, five timeboxed events, three artifacts, and one set of rules. The Scrum Guide states that modifying the format means you are not using Scrum. What remains variable is what you build. Scrum does not define technical or engineering practices, which is why teams can follow the schedule strictly without improving output quality.

**The Agile Manifesto standardizes values and principles.** It prioritizes individuals, working software, collaboration, and adaptation. Specific practices are considered temporary and flexible. Because it contains few specific rules, organizations frequently adopt the terminology without changing their management practices.

**The Improvement Kata standardizes problem-solving steps.** It uses a four-step cycle: establish the direction, assess the current situation, set the next target state, and run experiments. It standardizes the method for making improvements, not the daily work itself. Goals and experiments change, but the scientific method remains constant.

**Lean and the Toyota Production System standardize the flow of work.** They define value streams, visual signals (kanban), and takt time. The specific tasks and operational decisions stay flexible. In Lean systems, a standard exists to make operational problems visible. When work deviates from the standard, the team investigates the root cause.

**Kanban standardizes visual tracking and process constraints.** It fixes the visual board, work-in-progress (WIP) limits, and explicit operating policies. The actual process adapts over time. Policies are treated as temporary rules to be reviewed and updated when work flow slows down.

**Cynefin standardizes context assessment.** It requires teams to diagnose their environment—Clear, Complicated, Complex, or Chaotic—before choosing an approach. Metrics, procedures, and targets depend on the domain. It standardizes how managers analyze situations, allowing execution methods to change based on context.

## Comparing the Six Traditions

|Framework|What is fixed|What remains flexible|Definition of "agile"|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Scrum**|Operating format (roles, events, artifacts, rules)|Product scope and technical execution|A repeating schedule that requires regular reviews|
|**Agile Manifesto**|Core values and 12 principles|Roles, scope, tools, and practices|An adaptive stance rather than a specific process|
|**Improvement Kata**|Problem-solving method|Goals, experiments, and daily tasks|Standardizing how you change work, not how you execute it|
|**Lean / TPS**|Value streams and error signals|Task execution and worker decisions|Exposing operational problems so they can be fixed|
|**Kanban**|Visual board and WIP limits|Roles, schedules, and specific processes|Managing work flow using explicit constraints|
|**Cynefin**|Context diagnosis|Methods, outcomes, and definitions of success|Matching management methods to operational complexity|

These frameworks are choices about where to create stability in an organization. Standardizing meetings without understanding these differences creates administrative overhead without improving output.

## How Rules Behave When Broken

Instead of classifying frameworks as "heavy" or "lightweight," evaluate what happens when a team breaks a standard rule.

When a team intentionally violates a rule during an iteration, the organization responds in one of two ways:

- The system treats the violation as operational data to analyze (_"Why did this happen?"_).
- The system treats the violation as a policy failure to correct (_"You are not following the process"_).

Here is how each framework handles rule violations:

- **Lean treats violations as signals.** Standards exist to highlight problems. A broken standard indicates operational stress that requires investigation.
- **The Kata requires planned disruption.** Experiments deliberately change current routines to test if a new method achieves the target state.
- **Kanban treats rules as changeable policies.** If a WIP limit or policy impedes work flow, the team changes the policy.
- **The Manifesto is too broad to violate easily.** High-level values like "responding to change" are difficult to measure or enforce objectively.
- **Scrum enforces structural rules.** Changing Scrum events or roles is classified as incorrect application of the framework rather than an operational experiment.
- **Cynefin changes rules by environment.** Using rigid procedures in a Complex domain is an error in assessing context.

## Why Variable Space Is Necessary for Operations

Teams need operational flexibility to address unexpected problems.

If every detail of a team's day is standardized, they cannot adapt to unpredicted operational issues. Standardizing every step limits a team's ability to identify process improvements.

Standardizing core constraints provides stability so teams can experiment safely within set boundaries. For example, standard work at Toyota provides a clear baseline so workers can identify and test potential improvements.

In complex environments where outcomes cannot be predicted in advance, managers cannot design a complete process ahead of time. Standards should guide how teams adapt to conditions rather than prohibit changes.

## Applying Standards to Daily Practices

This distinction applies to small daily practices as well as entire frameworks. Consider how organizations standardize user stories:

**Standardizing template syntax:** Organizations often mandate a specific sentence format (_"As a \[role], I want \[action], so that \[benefit]"_) and check tickets for compliance. The original purpose of the format was to prompt three specific discussions: who needs the feature, what they are doing, and why it matters. Mandating the format often leads teams to fill out the template to satisfy management requirements without holding the necessary discussions.

**Standardizing the 3Cs model:** The 3Cs model (Card, Conversation, Confirmation) defines the purpose of a user story rather than its phrasing. The Card is a reminder to hold a discussion. The Conversation is where the details are defined between the team and the stakeholder. Confirmation defines acceptance criteria through specific test cases.

Comparing the two approaches when a step is skipped:

- If a team skips the discussion but fills out the mandatory template correctly, a syntax-based standard detects no issue. The feature is built and shipped before anyone realizes it was unnecessary.
- Under the 3Cs approach, skipping the discussion invalidates the user story immediately, regardless of what is written down.

## Standardizing Ceremonies Versus Standardizing Systems

Organizations often attempt to adopt agile methods by standardizing visible activities—most commonly by mandating Scrum events across all teams. They apply this schedule to teams whose main operational problems require Lean value-stream design or Cynefin context analysis.

This approach creates administrative friction. It standardizes meeting schedules while leaving underlying operational bottlenecks unaddressed, resulting in fixed calendars without improved team decision-making.

When multiple teams require shared standards, Kanban or the Improvement Kata are often more practical starting points:

- **Kanban** standardizes visual management and work-in-progress policies across team dependencies while allowing local teams to keep their existing workflows.
- **The Improvement Kata** standardizes how teams analyze and solve problems without dictating their daily task execution.

Standardizing the method for organizational learning allows daily execution practices to remain flexible.

## Choosing Which Level to Standardize

When introducing an organizational standard, select **one** primary system level to fix: values, operational schedules, work-flow limits, problem-solving methods, or context-assessment rules.

Keep the remaining system levels flexible, and use contextual frameworks like Cynefin to guide operational choices as conditions change.

If an agile adoption initiative consists primarily of mandating specific meetings across teams, the organization has standardized administrative routines rather than operational systems.

Standards should provide a stable base for operations rather than a fixed set of restrictions on daily work.
